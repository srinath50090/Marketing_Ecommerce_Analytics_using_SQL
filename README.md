# E-commerce Business Analytics Using SQL

**Turning E-commerce Data into Business Insights with MySQL**

An end-to-end SQL analytics project focused on understanding revenue performance, customer purchasing behavior, product performance, marketing campaigns, market trends, and business risks.

This project demonstrates how SQL can be used to investigate real-world business questions, identify performance gaps, measure revenue concentration, and investigate the potential causes of declining business performance.

## Project Overview

E-commerce businesses generate large amounts of customer, transaction, product, and marketing data. However, raw data alone cannot explain why revenue changes, which customers drive business growth, which products underperform, or where potential risks exist.

In this project, I used MySQL to analyze e-commerce data across multiple business dimensions. I structured the analysis around business questions rather than simply writing queries to retrieve data.

The objective was to move beyond basic reporting and develop SQL queries that support business performance monitoring, customer analysis, marketing evaluation, and root-cause investigation.

## Business Problems Solved

### 1. Revenue & Sales Analysis
- Identify the highest revenue-generating countries, categories, brands, and products.
- Calculate revenue contribution and average order value (AOV).
- Compare revenue performance across customer age groups and markets.
- Measure month-over-month revenue growth and decline.
- Identify products and markets experiencing the largest revenue drops.

### 2. Customer Behavior & Retention Analysis
- Identify customers who have never purchased and customers who purchased only once.
- Calculate repeat customer percentage and repeat purchase rate.
- Analyze customer lifetime revenue and purchasing frequency.
- Identify customers who have not purchased recently.
- Investigate customers with high purchase frequency but low revenue, and vice versa.
- Compare customer purchasing behavior across countries and age groups.

### 3. Product & Category Performance
- Rank top-performing products and brands within categories and countries.
- Identify products popular across multiple markets and age groups.
- Compare order volume with revenue to identify potential underperforming products.
- Measure product contribution to category revenue.
- Analyze product, brand, and category refund performance.

### 4. Marketing & Campaign Analysis
- Compare campaign channels based on revenue and order volume.
- Identify campaigns generating the highest revenue per customer.
- Calculate campaign conversion rates using transaction and session data.
- Compare conversion performance across countries, acquisition channels, and product categories.
- Identify campaigns with declining revenue performance over time.

### 5. Country & Market Analysis
- Compare countries based on revenue per customer and average order value.
- Identify markets with high repeat purchase rates and refund rates.
- Analyze average month-over-month revenue growth by country.
- Identify dominant product categories in each market.
- Find markets with a strong purchasing customer base but relatively low revenue.

### 6. Time-Series & Trend Analysis
- Analyze monthly revenue and AOV trends.
- Identify months with the highest revenue growth and largest declines.
- Calculate three-month moving averages and running revenue totals.
- Identify products and countries with significant revenue declines.
- Investigate consistent month-over-month revenue growth patterns.

### 7. Revenue Concentration & Business Risk
- Measure revenue contribution from top products, categories, and countries.
- Analyze how much revenue is generated by a small group of customers.
- Identify categories dependent on a small number of products.
- Identify countries dependent on a small number of categories.
- Evaluate potential business risks associated with concentrated revenue sources.

### 8. Root-Cause Analysis
- Investigate which countries and categories contribute most to revenue declines.
- Identify products responsible for declining revenue within individual countries.
- Investigate which brands contribute to category-level revenue declines.
- Identify products or categories where order volume increases while revenue decreases.
- Investigate why customer numbers can increase while revenue per customer declines.
- Identify categories responsible for increases in refunds.
- Evaluate whether a dominant product category creates excessive business dependency.

## Data Quality & Preparation

Before performing the analysis, I examined the dataset structure and documented important data quality issues that could affect business conclusions.

Key activities included:

- Creating SQL tables for campaigns, customers, events, products, and transactions.
- Investigating missing product IDs in event and transaction records.
- Creating `events_vw` to exclude purchase events with missing product IDs while retaining other events.
- Creating `transactions_vw` to exclude records with missing product IDs and records flagged as refunds from the primary transaction analysis.
- Documenting missing campaign names, campaign costs, customer details, and product descriptions.

These checks help establish consistent analytical datasets and make the assumptions behind the analysis more transparent.

**Important:** Excluding refund records from the primary transaction view is an analytical choice, not a complete reconciliation of sales and refunds. Refund analysis uses the underlying transaction data separately.

## Analytical Approach

The project follows a structured workflow:

1. **Understand the data:** Examine table structures, columns, and relationships.
2. **Check data quality:** Investigate missing values and records that could distort analysis.
3. **Prepare analytical views:** Apply documented filtering rules for the primary analysis.
4. **Define business questions:** Translate business problems into measurable SQL queries.
5. **Calculate KPIs:** Measure revenue, AOV, repeat purchasing, conversion, growth, and concentration.
6. **Compare performance:** Rank products, customers, categories, campaigns, and countries.
7. **Investigate changes:** Trace revenue declines and other performance gaps to more detailed business segments.
8. **Support decisions:** Use the results to identify areas that require further investigation or potential business action.

## Business Value

The analysis is designed to help answer questions such as:

- Which products, categories, and markets contribute most to revenue?
- Are customers returning to make repeat purchases?
- Which customer segments generate higher revenue per customer?
- Which campaign channels generate stronger revenue and conversion performance?
- Is revenue overly dependent on a few products, categories, countries, or customers?
- Where should a business investigate when revenue declines?
- Why might order volume grow while revenue falls?
- Which markets or customer segments need further investigation?

These questions connect SQL querying with practical business analysis and decision support.

## Data Limitations & Assumptions

The available data has some limitations that affect how the results should be interpreted.

- Product names and descriptions are unavailable, so products are identified using `product_id`.
- Campaign names and campaign cost information are unavailable, limiting campaign-level interpretation and preventing a reliable profitability or ROI calculation from the available campaign data alone.
- Some transaction records have missing product IDs.
- Refund records are filtered out of the primary transaction view because corresponding original purchase records are not consistently available in the dataset.
- Conversion calculations depend on the definition of a session and the relationship between event records and transaction records.
- Revenue concentration thresholds and business risk classifications are analytical definitions used in this project, not universal industry standards.

The results should therefore be interpreted within these data constraints, and the assumptions should be validated before using the analysis for actual business decisions.
